Irish Study FAQ: Questions Self-Taught Learners Ask

2026-08-09

How long does it take to learn Irish?

This depends entirely on your definition of "learn" and how intensely you study. The U.S. Foreign Service Institute estimates that English speakers need roughly 1,100 hours of study to reach professional working proficiency in Irish. If you study 10 hours per week, that's about two years. At 5 hours per week, it's four years. At 15 hours per week, it's roughly 18 months. These are ballpark figures; some people move faster, others slower. The key insight is that Irish is a moderately difficult language for English speakers (more complex than Romance languages, but less complex than tonal languages or languages with entirely different writing systems), and time plus consistency are your friends.

Should I focus on one Irish dialect, or learn multiple?

Irish has three main regional dialects: Connacht (Conamara), Ulster (Donegal), and Munster (Cork/Kerry). They differ in pronunciation, some vocabulary, and some grammatical features. Beginners are often paralyzed by this choice. The answer: pick one and stick with it initially. Most resources default to Connacht Irish, which is a sensible starting point. Once you reach B1 or B2 level, expose yourself to other dialects through listening (podcasts, films, conversation partners). The dialects are mutually intelligible; learning one gives you the foundation to understand the others. Don't let dialect anxiety stop you from starting.

Is Irish grammar really that hard?

Irish grammar is different from English, not necessarily harder. The key quirks English speakers stumble on:

  • Verb-Subject-Object word order: Irish is a VSO language (verb comes first), not SVO like English. "Ith mé an t-úachta" literally means "Ate I the ice cream," not "I ate the ice cream." This feels backward until you get used to it—usually a few weeks of regular practice.
  • Mutations: Irish words change based on grammar context (lenition, eclipsis, h-prefixing). "Bean" (woman) becomes "bhean" in certain contexts. This is the most infamous Irish grammar feature, but it's learnable through practice and pattern recognition, not memorization.
  • Prepositional pronouns: Prepositions combine with pronouns into single words. Instead of "to me," Irish has "dom" (to me) as one word. This actually simplifies things compared to English once you learn the patterns.

The takeaway: Irish grammar is different and requires consistent practice to internalize, but it's not impossible. Thousands of non-native speakers reach C1 and C2 levels every year.

What's the best way to practice speaking if I don't have Irish speakers around me?

You have more options than you might think:

  • Language exchange apps: Tandem, ConversationExchange, and similar platforms connect you with native Irish speakers who want to learn English. You typically exchange 30 minutes of Irish practice for 30 minutes of English teaching. These conversations are often supportive and fun.
  • Online tutors: Preply and other platforms offer affordable Irish lessons. Hiring a native speaker for even one 30-minute lesson per week gives you structured speaking practice and real-time feedback.
  • Solo speaking practice: Record yourself speaking in Irish for 5-10 minutes about your day. Describe what you're doing right now. Narrate a simple story. This builds fluency and confidence, even without a listener.
  • Language learning communities: Reddit's r/LearnIrish, Discord servers, and Facebook groups for Irish learners often organize voice chat sessions and conversation practice.

How much vocabulary do I need to learn?

You need far less vocabulary than you think to function in Irish. A1 level requires roughly 500-1,000 words. A2 requires 1,000-1,500. B1 requires 1,500-2,500. B2 requires 2,500-3,500. C1 requires 3,500-5,000. You don't need a huge vocabulary to communicate; you need the right vocabulary and the ability to use it accurately. It's better to know 1,000 words very well (including how they combine and transform) than to vaguely recognize 5,000 words. Focus on high-frequency words first, then expand gradually.

Is it worth buying learning apps and courses, or should I just use free resources?

Both can work, and many learners use a combination. Free resources (YouTube channels like Seachtain na Gaeilge, the BBC Learning Irish course, podcasts like "Easy Irish") are extensive and genuinely high-quality. Paid apps (Duolingo, Babbel) offer structure, gamification, and personalized learning paths, which some people find helpful for habit-building. The truth: the best resource is the one you'll actually use consistently. If a $10-15/month app keeps you accountable, it's worth it. If free YouTube videos work for you, there's no need to spend money. Experiment and see what clicks.

How do I avoid the "intermediate plateau" where progress stalls?

Many learners hit a frustrating plateau around A2-B1 level where progress feels slower and learning feels less rewarding. You're past the exciting beginner stage where every new word feels significant, but you're not yet fluent enough to have real conversations. This plateau is real and common. To break through it:

  • Increase your study intensity and focus on speaking and writing (the productive skills that often get neglected)
  • Engage with native-speaker content (podcasts, films, books) without translation aids—force yourself to understand from context
  • Find an Irish language community (online or in-person) for regular conversation practice
  • Set a specific goal, like passing TEG B1 or having a 10-minute conversation with a native speaker
  • Track your progress (vocabulary growth, speaking comfort, comprehension improvements) to remind yourself that you ARE making progress, even if it feels slower than the beginning

Should I hire a tutor, or can I learn entirely self-taught?

Self-taught learning is absolutely possible—thousands of people have reached B2 and C1 levels without formal tutoring. However, a tutor provides irreplaceable value: immediate feedback on your pronunciation and grammar, conversation practice with someone invested in your learning, and personalized guidance based on your specific needs. A tutor doesn't replace self-study; they accelerate and enhance it. If your budget allows for even one lesson per week or per month, it's worth the investment. If not, self-taught learning with language exchange partners can work perfectly well.

What if I'm worried I'll never reach conversational fluency?

This worry is common and understandable. But research is clear: consistent practice over time produces fluency. The question isn't "can I reach fluency?" (you can—others have). The question is "am I willing to show up regularly?" If you study Irish for 30-60 minutes most days for 12-18 months, focusing on all four skills and including speaking practice, you will reach conversational fluency. There's nothing magical about this. It's just effort plus time. Track your progress, adjust your methods based on data, and keep going.

How do I stay motivated for the long term?

Long-term motivation comes from connection, not willpower. The learners who stick with Irish for years are those who connect it to something meaningful—family heritage, cultural pride, a dream of visiting Ireland, or simply loving how the language sounds and feels. Motivation also comes from visible progress. This is why tracking matters. When you can see that you've studied 200 hours, learned 1,500 words, and held several conversations in Irish, your brain says "this is working," and motivation strengthens.

Practical strategies: join a community of Irish learners, listen to music and podcasts you genuinely enjoy in Irish (not just "for learning"), plan a trip to Ireland, consider taking the TEG exam as a concrete goal, and use a tracker to make your progress visible. Small systems matter more than big motivation.
