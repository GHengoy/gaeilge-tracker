Key takeaways

  • Rough targets: about 100-200 words for greetings and survival phrases, 500-1,000 for a basic conversation, and 1,500-2,500 for comfortable everyday chat.
  • Frequency beats size: the most common few hundred words cover most of what you'll actually hear and say, so quality of word choice matters more than raw count.
  • Active and passive are different numbers: words you can produce on demand are a smaller, harder-won set than words you merely recognize.
  • Track growth, not just a final number: logging new words as you learn them shows real momentum long before you hit any milestone.
  • Move the goalpost as you go: once a target feels comfortable, set the next one rather than treating any single number as "done."

The Short Answer: Rough Word Counts by Goal

Every Irish learner eventually asks some version of this question, usually while staring at a flashcard deck wondering if they'll ever "know enough." There's no single magic number, but there are useful ranges depending on what you actually want to do with the language.

GoalRough active vocabulary needed
Greetings and survival phrases100-200 words
Basic conversation about familiar topics500-1,000 words
Comfortable everyday chat1,500-2,500 words
TEG A2-B1 level conversationroughly 2,000-3,000 words
Fluent, nuanced discussion4,000+ words, growing for years

These ranges line up with what frequency research shows across most languages: a relatively small core vocabulary covers a surprisingly large share of everyday speech, and each step up toward fluency costs more words for less additional coverage.

Why Word Count Alone Can Mislead You

Two learners can both "know" 600 words and be at very different levels. One learner picked up 600 high-frequency words: common verbs, question words, everyday nouns. The other learned 600 words from a vocabulary app's random lesson order, including plenty of rarely-used terms they'll go months without hitting again.

The first learner can actually hold a conversation. The second has a longer flashcard streak and a weaker one. Raw count is a rough proxy for ability, not a guarantee of it.

Coverage, not just count

Research on frequency dictionaries across languages consistently shows the same pattern: the most common 500-1,000 words typically cover 80-90% of ordinary spoken conversation. The next few thousand words add nuance and precision, but with steeply diminishing returns per word learned.

What to Learn First: The High-Frequency Core

If you're building toward a basic conversation target, prioritize these categories before anything specialized:

  • Core verbs: bí (be), déan (do/make), abair (say), téigh (go), faigh (get), feic (see) — a handful of verbs carry most everyday sentences.
  • Question words: cad/céard (what), cé (who), cá (where), cathain (when), cén fáth (why) — these unlock conversation rather than monologue.
  • Pronouns and conjugated prepositions: agam, agat, aige, aici and similar forms, since Irish folds pronoun meaning into the preposition itself.
  • Numbers and time words: counting, days, and basic time expressions come up in nearly every real exchange.
  • Connectors: agus (and), ach (but), mar (because) — small words that turn a list of phrases into actual conversation.

Learning fifty specialized nouns about, say, weather or food before you've nailed these categories is a common ordering mistake. It feels productive, but it doesn't move your conversational ability nearly as much as finishing the core set first.

Active vs. Passive Vocabulary: Two Different Numbers

Vocabulary size questions almost always conflate two different skills. Your passive vocabulary is everything you recognize when you hear or read it. Your active vocabulary is the smaller set you can retrieve and produce correctly, under time pressure, without a dictionary.

Passive vocabulary grows faster and is typically two to three times larger than active vocabulary at any given stage. This gap is normal, not a sign you're doing something wrong. It does mean that if your goal is holding a conversation rather than understanding a podcast, active vocabulary is the number worth tracking and deliberately pushing.

Test the gap yourself

Pick twenty words from a recent study session. Can you recall and use each one out loud, unprompted, in a full sentence? The ones you hesitate on or can't produce at all are still passive, no matter how many times you've seen them on a flashcard.

A Simple Way to Track Your Growing Vocabulary

Most learners have no idea how many words they actually know, because nobody keeps a running count. That's a solvable problem with a basic logging habit:

  1. Log new words as you meet them. Add a quick vocabulary count to your daily study entry rather than trying to reconstruct a total from memory later.
  2. Separate "introduced" from "active." A word you've seen once isn't the same as one you can produce correctly a week later. Move words to "active" only once you've recalled them unprompted a few times.
  3. Review your running total weekly. Watching the number climb, even slowly, is tangible proof that counters the feeling of "I'm not getting anywhere."
  4. Note the category. A quick tag (verbs, food, family terms, exam vocabulary) lets you spot gaps, like realizing you know forty food words but almost no connectors.

This doesn't need to be complicated. A spreadsheet column that increments as you log sessions is enough to turn a vague sense of progress into an actual, defensible number.

Setting Your Own Vocabulary Milestone

Rather than fixating on a single target number pulled from an article (including this one), set a milestone based on where you are right now:

  • If you're under 200 active words, aim for 500 as your next checkpoint, focused entirely on the high-frequency core above.
  • If you're around 500-800, aim for 1,500 and start branching into topics you personally care about, since motivation-driven vocabulary sticks better than generic lists.
  • If you're past 1,500, shift focus from adding brand-new words to deepening ones you already half-know, since at this stage grammar and fluency of retrieval matter as much as raw count.

Once a milestone stops feeling like a stretch, that's your signal to set the next one. Vocabulary growth for most learners isn't linear and doesn't need to be; it just needs a record showing it's happening at all.
