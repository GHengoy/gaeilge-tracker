Introduction: A Tool Built for Real Learners

The Irish Gaelic Self-Study Progress Tracker is designed for self-taught learners of all motivations—heritage learners reconnecting with their culture, hobbyists pursuing Irish for love of the language, and examination candidates preparing for the Teastas Eorpach na Gaeilge (TEG). The core columns apply to everyone. Optional columns support specific use cases. This guide walks you through the tracker's structure and how to use it effectively.

Core Columns: Everyone Logs These

Every learning session gets logged with the same core fields. These apply whether you're doing a casual study hour or intensive exam prep:

  • Date: The day you studied. This seems obvious, but consistency matters—log the day you actually studied, not the day you plan to study.
  • Study Hours: Total hours spent learning Irish on that day. If you studied for 45 minutes, log 0.75. Fractional hours matter; they accumulate into measurable progress.
  • Vocabulary Words Learned: New words or phrases you encountered and learned today. If you reviewed 30 flashcards, log 30. If you learned 5 new words in a reading session, log 5. Don't count words you already knew. The goal is tracking active vocabulary growth.
  • Grammar Patterns Covered: Irish grammar topics you practiced or studied. Did you work on conditional mutations? Log that. Did you practice past tense verbs? Log that. This helps you see whether you're systematically covering the language's structure or randomly jumping around.
  • Listening Practice Minutes: Total minutes spent on listening—podcasts, audiobooks, videos, music, conversation. Track it separately to monitor your receptive skill development.
  • Speaking Practice Minutes: Total minutes spent speaking—conversation with partners, language exchange apps, reading aloud, voice messages. This matters even if it feels awkward; tracking forces you to ensure it happens.
  • Writing Practice Minutes: Total minutes spent writing—journaling, forum posts, language exchange messages, essays. Productive skill time is often underestimated; tracking makes it visible.
  • Reading Practice Minutes: Total minutes spent reading—news, books, social media, graded readers. Like listening, reading can feel productive without being logged; tracking reveals whether you're actually doing it.
  • Study Streak: The number of consecutive days you've logged study (even your minimum routine counts). This is purely motivational—seeing a streak of 15 or 30 days reinforces that you're consistent.
  • Notes: Any observations about your session. Did you have a breakthrough? Was today harder than usual? Did you have a conversation with a native speaker? Notes provide qualitative color to your quantitative data.

Optional Columns: For Heritage and Diaspora Learners

If you're a heritage learner or diaspora learner reconnecting with Irish through family or cultural roots, these columns capture what matters most to you:

  • Cultural Motivation Note: Why are you learning Irish today? Is it to strengthen family bonds? To participate in cultural events? To pass knowledge to your children? Logging your motivation reminds you why you started and reconnects each session to your bigger purpose.
  • Family Stories Practiced: Did you learn or practice a family story, saying, or tradition in Irish? These are often the most meaningful and memorable words learners acquire. Tracking them acknowledges their importance beyond the vocabulary count.
  • Conversation with Native Speaker Minutes: Total minutes spent speaking Irish with actual native speakers—family members, friends, language exchange partners. This is distinct from recorded practice or solo speaking; it's real-time interaction. Tracking this reveals how often you're actually using Irish with people who speak it natively.

Optional Columns: For TEG Exam Candidates

If you're preparing for the Teastas Eorpach na Gaeilge (TEG) certification exam, these columns guide your preparation:

  • TEG Level Target: Which level are you aiming for? A1 (Absolute Beginner), A2 (Elementary), B1 (Intermediate), B2 (Upper Intermediate), C1 (Advanced), or C2 (Mastery)? Knowing your target level shapes what you study.
  • TEG Section Target: Which exam component are you focusing on today? TEG exams include listening, reading, writing, and speaking components. Logging your focus ensures you're preparing comprehensively rather than avoiding weak areas.
  • Mock Exam Score: If you take practice exams, log your score and the date. Over time, this reveals whether your score is trending upward, plateauing, or declining—critical information for adjusting your study strategy with weeks to spare.
  • Days to TEG Exam: How many days until your scheduled exam? This is motivational and practical. As the date approaches, you can see whether your study intensity matches your timeline. Eight weeks out? You should be in steady preparation mode. Two weeks out? You should be in high-intensity review and mock exams.
  • Target Completion Percentage: Set a realistic target for exam performance (e.g., 75% of marks, 80%, 90%). Track this as a reminder of what you're working toward. As exam day nears, use your actual mock exam scores to refine this target.

How to Log Effectively

Daily logging: Ideally, log your study session the same day, while the numbers are fresh. If that doesn't work, add a weekly review session where you estimate your hours, minutes, and vocabulary for the past week and log it all at once. Accuracy is better than immediacy; it's fine to backfill a week at a time.

Be honest about numbers: Don't inflate your hours or vocabulary counts. The purpose of tracking is to give you real data, not to make yourself feel good with inflated numbers. If you studied for 30 minutes, log 0.5 hours. If you learned three words, log three. Real numbers reveal real patterns.

Log your minimum sessions too: On days when you only do your 15-minute minimum routine, log it. This isn't a failure; it's consistency. A month of 15-minute sessions is 7.5 hours of study—meaningful progress. Tracking shows that consistency matters more than any single ambitious day.

Reading Your Data: Monthly and Quarterly Reviews

Once you've logged for at least a month, take time to review:

  • What was your average daily study time? Your weekly study time?
  • Which skill did you spend the most time on? Which got neglected?
  • How many new vocabulary words did you acquire this month?
  • Is your study streak growing? When do you most often miss days?
  • If you're exam-prepping, what's your mock exam score trend?

These patterns guide your next month. Maybe you'll see that you're weak on speaking and can prioritize conversation exchanges. Maybe you'll notice you study best in the morning and can schedule accordingly. Maybe you'll see that vocabulary growth is slower than you'd like and can shift your approach. The data teaches you about yourself as a learner.
